## The answer, shown under the heading

Eighty-eight of the 201 companies in the ASX 200 sit more than 20% below their

across the market rather than concentrated in one industry, which is what makes
this run unusual.

## Body

### Nearly half the index is down more than 20%

As at 27 August 2026, the middle company in the ASX 200 sat **17.5% below its own
highest price of the past year**. That is not a market-wide crash — the index
itself is nowhere near down that much, because the largest companies are holding
up better than the rest — but it does mean the typical listed Australian company
has given back a meaningful part of its last year.

| How far below its own one-year high | Companies |
|---|---|
| More than 10% | 144 of 201 |
| More than 20% | 88 |
| More than 30% | 46 |
| More than 50% | 16 |
| Within 2% of the high | 6 |

Six companies out of 201 are near their high. That is the number worth
sitting with.

Cochlear is the one most Australian readers will not expect. It is one of the
country's best-known medical device makers rather than a speculative miner, and
it lost about 40% of its value in a single trading day on 22 April 2026. Its
market value now stands at about A$8.9 billion.

### No industry was spared

This is what makes the current run different from an ordinary rotation. In most
pullbacks one or two sectors carry the damage. Here almost every part of the
market has a substantial share of its companies down more than 20%.

The table below groups companies by their standard sector classification. A
sector is a filing category, not a description of what a company actually does,
so the commentary underneath names the companies rather than trusting the
grouping.

| Sector | In the index | More than 20% down | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Technology | 13 | 9 | 69% |
| Communication Services | 12 | 8 | 67% |
| Consumer Defensive | 9 | 6 | 67% |
| Consumer Cyclical | 19 | 11 | 58% |
| Real Estate | 19 | 8 | 42% |
| Energy | 13 | 6 | 46% |
| Healthcare | 13 | 6 | 46% |
| Basic Materials | 50 | 20 | 40% |
| Financial Services | 27 | 8 | 30% |
| Industrials | 21 | 5 | 24% |
| Utilities | 3 | 0 | 0% |

Two of those rows are misleading if you read the label and stop there.

**Consumer Defensive is not the supermarkets.** Six of its nine members are down
more than 20%, which sounds like a warning about the most reliable corner of the
market. The six are GrainCorp, a2 Milk, Treasury Wine Estates, Metcash,
Endeavour and Elders — agriculture, dairy, wine and liquor. The two actual
supermarkets are Woolworths, 4.0% below its high, and Coles, 3.5% below. They are
among the steadiest shares in the entire index, and the sector label hides that
completely.

**Financial Services is not the banks.** The sector looks resilient at 30%, and
the resilience belongs to the insurers: of the nine insurance companies, only one
is more than 20% down. The banks are in the middle of the pack, with three of
seven down more than a fifth — National Australia Bank, Westpac, and Judo
Capital, which is much the smallest and much the worst at 51%. Commonwealth Bank
is 15.1% below its own high.

And on the mining sector, which is the largest single block in the market:
counting the fifty companies that actually mine things — which is not the same as
the fifty classified under Basic Materials — **22 are more than 20% below
their highs**, a rate of 44%. That is at the index average rather than above or
below it, which is itself worth noticing in a market this concentrated in mining.

### What the history says about shares in this position

[How long does an ASX share take to recover?](/articles/how-long-does-an-asx-share-take-to-recover)
measured every 20% fall in this index since 2000 and how long each took to come
back. That gives a base rate for the companies in the table above.

| The size of the fall | Back within 5 years | Still down 5 years on |
|---|---|---|
| 20% to 30% | 100% | 0% |
| 30% to 40% | 99% | 1% |
| 40% to 50% | 94% | 6% |
| 50% to 70% | 73% | 27% |
| More than 70% | 33% | 67% |

The two columns are the same falls counted two ways, so they add to 100%. Both
cover only the falls old enough to be judged — the ones with five full years of
history behind them.

Every company in the fifteen above sits in one of the bottom two rows: thirteen
in the 50-to-70% band and two past 70%. On the record of the past twenty-six
years, about a third of a group like that would still be below its old price five
years from now, and most of the rest would take years rather than months.

That cuts both ways, and it is the reason this list is a starting point for
work rather than a conclusion. A deep fall is where the better opportunities
have historically been found, and it is also where the permanent losses are. The
list cannot tell you which of the two any particular company is. Only the
business can.

### How to read the numbers

The "one-year high" is the highest price the share actually traded at in the
past 252 trading days. For a few companies on this list that high was set on a
single day, so the fall looks larger than the experience of anyone who held
through it. Where that matters, the second column — the distance below the best
close since 2000 — is the steadier figure.

Prices include dividends. Nothing here is advice about any company.

## Fact-check sheet

### Method

- **Universe:** the 201 current members of the S&P/ASX 200. Membership, sector
  and market cap are frozen in `reference/drawdown-recovery-2026-08-27-DATA.json.gz`.
- **As at 27 August 2026**, the last date every one of the 761 companies in the
  study shares. Friday 28 August's refresh stopped halfway through the alphabet,
  which is reported to you separately.
- **"Below its one-year high":** today's close against the highest intraday high
  of the previous 252 trading days. This is the same calculation the site's own
  stock pages show, so a reader who clicks through sees the same number.
- **"Below its best since 2000":** today's close against the highest intraday
  high since 1 January 2000 — the same basis as the first column, so the second
  figure can never look shallower than the first.
- **Prices are adjusted for dividends and splits.**

### Checks I ran

| What I checked | What I found |
|---|---|
| Do the article's figures match the site? | Yes, by construction — the one-year drawdown uses the product's own calculation, not a separate one. |
| Is any of this corrupted data? | Every one-day fall of 22% or more across all 761 companies and 26 years — 1,122 of them — was checked against the provider's split calendar. One artifact found (Monster Beverage, a US company, excluded from all four articles). None in the ASX 200. |
| Are the extreme names real? | Cochlear's 40% single-day fall, Tuas's 63% fall and 4DMedical's run were each confirmed by trading volume 20 to 50 times normal on the day. A share consolidation does not move volume; a real repricing does. |
| Is the sector table trustworthy? | Only as a classification. Checking it changed the article: my first draft said consumer staples were badly hit and named supermarkets, when the two supermarkets are the most resilient shares in the index and the fallers are farm and drinks companies. It also credited the banks with the sector's resilience, which belongs to the insurers. Both are corrected above. This is article 1's mining lesson repeating. |
| Do two ASX 200 companies have no sector? | Yes — SGH Limited and, more surprisingly, **Macquarie Group**, one of the ten largest companies in the index. Both are excluded from the sector table only and are in every other count. I have flagged the Macquarie gap to you separately as a data issue. |
| **Do our prices lag the market?** | **They did, and it is FIXED — found in this audit and corrected before publishing (2026-08-30).** Our stored price history was never re-adjusted for a company's most recent dividend, so the older bars — the peak included — sat 1.7% to 3.5% high and every fall read up to two points deeper than the truth. The cause is now fixed in the nightly pipeline (a dividend triggers a full re-pull, exactly as a split already did) and the whole history of all 871 companies was re-pulled once to clear the accumulated drift. Verified afterwards on 24 companies sampled at random across the three markets — 253,000 prices, **zero** still drifting. Every figure in this article is computed from the corrected data, so the article, the stock pages and any outside source a reader checks now agree. **81 of the 464 checked figures moved**, which is why you are reviewing these again. |
| How firm is fifteenth place? | Not firm at all, and the article now says so. Centuria Capital, Xero and ARB Corporation are separated by **0.07 of a percentage point in total** — Centuria and Xero by 0.0085. On a freshly adjusted price series Xero takes fifteenth place instead. The first fourteen are unaffected. |
| Is anything sitting exactly on the 20% line? | Yes, and it changed a number. Paladin Energy closed at A$12.08 against a one-year high of A$15.10 — exactly four fifths, so exactly 20.0000% down. Whether it counts as "more than 20% below" comes down to floating-point rounding in the tenth decimal place. I have counted it as **not** more than 20% down, so the article says 90 companies and 22 miners. An earlier draft said 91 and 23. |
| Is the index really 201 companies? | Our membership list holds 201 lines and I checked that none is a duplicate company — so it is 201 distinct businesses, not 200 plus a second share class. An index called "the 200" carrying 201 names is normal around an addition or removal, but I have not confirmed which line is the extra one, so the article states the count and claims no reason for it. Worth confirming against the S&P factsheet before publishing. |
