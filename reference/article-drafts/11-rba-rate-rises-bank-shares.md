## The answer, shown under the heading

Across 17 RBA rate rises since 2022, the big four banks' shares barely moved on the day: the
average move was almost exactly zero, because the rise was usually expected. The big moves came on
the two days the RBA surprised the market. In 2026, the banks' own results moved their shares far
more than any decision did.

## Body

### The short version

- **Rates have gone up four times this year.** The Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) raised its
  cash rate in February, March, May and September 2026, from **3.60%** to **4.60%**. Counting the
  13 rises of 2022–23, that is **17 rises** since May 2022.
- **On the day of a rise, the banks barely moved.** We took the average of the four big banks'
  share prices on each decision day. They closed higher on **6** of the 17 days and lower on
  **11**, and the average move was **+0.04%**, close to nothing.
- **The reason: by 2:30pm the news was usually old.** The RBA announces at 2:30pm on decision
  day. When nearly everyone expects a rise, share prices have already adjusted in the days or
  weeks before, so the announcement itself changes little.
- **The big days were the surprises.** The banks' worst decision day (**−2.37%**) was in June 2022,
  when the RBA raised rates by more than expected. Their best (**+4.39%**) was in October 2022,
  when it raised by less than expected.
- **This year, each bank's own results mattered more than the RBA.** Westpac fell **5.88%** on the
  day of its August update and ANZ **2.84%** on its results day. No 2026 rate decision moved any of
  the four by as much.
- **Rate rises cut both ways for a bank.** All four say higher rates help them earn more on some
  of their customers' savings. All four also say competition for loans takes much of that back,
  and they have set more money aside in case borrowers struggle.

### Five terms used in this article

- **Cash rate:** the interest rate the RBA sets. Banks' own rates, on home loans and savings
  accounts, tend to follow it. It is now 4.60%.
- **Percentage point:** a rise from 4.35% to 4.60% is a rise of 0.25 percentage points. You will
  also see this called "25 basis points"; 100 basis points make one percentage point.
- **The big four:** Commonwealth Bank (CBA), Westpac, National Australia Bank (NAB) and ANZ,
  Australia's four largest banks.
- **ASX 200:** an index of about 200 of the largest companies on the Australian share market. When
  people say "the market rose 1%", they usually mean this index. The big four are part of it.
- **"Priced in":** when investors already expect something, they trade on that expectation
  before it happens, so the share price already reflects it. When it then happens, the price has
  little left to adjust.

### Why would interest rates matter to a bank?

A bank makes most of its money from the gap between the interest it earns and the interest it
pays. It pays interest to savers and to the investors it borrows from, and it earns interest on
the home loans and business loans it makes. That gap is called the **net interest margin**.

CBA's net interest margin in the year to June 2026 was **2.05%**. Put simply, for every $100 it
has lent out or invested, it kept about $2.05 over the year after paying interest on the money it
used. The other three banks' latest figures: Westpac **1.89%** (three months to June 2026), NAB
**1.81%** and ANZ **1.53%** (both the six months to March 2026).

When the RBA raises rates, three things happen to that margin, and they pull in different
directions:

1. **Some savings earn the bank more.** Part of the money customers keep in everyday accounts
   earns them little or no interest. The bank invests that money, and when rates go up it earns
   more on it. All four banks named this as a benefit in their latest results.
2. **Competition takes some of it back.** Banks compete hard for loans by offering lower rates,
   and savers move money into accounts that pay bonus interest. All four named competition as a
   pressure on their margin.
3. **More borrowers may struggle.** Higher repayments make it harder for some customers to keep
   up, so banks set money aside for loans that may not be repaid. These are called **provisions**,
   and they reduce profit.

Here is what each bank said about those three things in its latest results:

| Bank | Benefit from higher rates | Pressure | Money set aside |
|---|---|---|---|
| CBA (year to June 2026) | "higher earnings on replicating portfolio and capital hedges" | "largely offset by lower lending margins" | Loan impairment expense up 9% to $788 million; "arrears rose in some consumer portfolios" |
| Westpac (quarter to June 2026) | "benefits from the higher interest rate environment" | "competitive pressures in lending, deposit mix and a higher proportion of customers qualifying for saving bonus rates" | Provisions above its central forecast rose to $2.0 billion |
| NAB (half to March 2026) | "higher earnings from the deposit replicating portfolio" | "offset by lending competition" | An extra $300 million of forward-looking provisions, for "potential stress related to the Middle East conflict" |
| ANZ (half to March 2026) | Replicating portfolios added 0.02 percentage points to the margin, "benefiting from higher rates" | "continued Australia home loan pricing competition" | A $175 million charge for "the potential impacts of the Middle East conflict" |

(A "replicating portfolio" is the bank's name for the way it invests that low-interest savings
money.)

**What happened last time.** CBA's results show how these forces played out over the 2022–23
rises:

| CBA's results for | Net interest margin | Cash rate at the end of the period |
|---|---|---|
| Year to June 2022 | 1.90% | 0.85% |
| Six months to December 2022 | 2.10% | 3.10% |
| Year to June 2023 | 2.07% | 4.10% |
| Year to June 2024 | 1.99% | 4.35% |
| Year to June 2025 | 2.08% | 3.85% |
| Year to June 2026 | 2.05% | 4.35% |

The first rises lifted CBA's margin by about 0.2 percentage points within six months. After that,
rates kept rising but the margin did not: CBA said its monthly margins peaked in late 2022 and
blamed competition for home loans and customers switching their savings. So higher rates helped,
but only for a while, and not by much.

For each of the 17 rises, we compared each bank's closing share price on decision day with its
closing price the trading day before. The RBA announces at 2:30pm Sydney time and the ASX's
trading day ends just after 4pm, so the decision-day close includes the market's reaction.

The table's columns:

- **Decision:** the day the RBA announced the rise.

- **New cash rate:** the cash rate after the rise.
- **Big four banks:** how much the four banks' share prices moved that day, on average. A minus
  sign means they fell.
- **ASX 200:** how much the whole market moved that day, for comparison.

**What the table shows:**

- The banks fell on 11 of the 17 days and rose on 6. A rate rise was not reliably good or bad
  news for their shares on the day.
- On 11 of the 17 days the banks moved by less than 1% either way, and on 15 of the 17 they moved
  in the same direction as the market.
- The banks did better than the market on 9 days and worse on 8, so a rise did not reliably favour
  them over other companies either.
- The reactions look much the same whether rates were rising from near zero, as in 2022, or
  from 3.60%, as in 2026.

### Why the day itself is usually quiet

By 2:30pm on decision day, the news was usually out. September 2026 shows how.

- **On Tuesday 8 September,** Westpac's economists said they now expected the RBA to raise rates
  again this year, which meant all four big banks' economists did, the Motley Fool reported. That day the four
  banks' shares fell **1.77%** on average and the ASX 200 fell **1.00%**.
- **The day before the decision,** the ABC reported that markets put the chance of a rise at about
  90%, and that all 29 economists surveyed by Bloomberg expected one.
- **On Tuesday 29 September,** the RBA raised the cash rate to 4.60%. The four banks' shares moved
  **−0.21%** on average that day, while the ASX 200 rose 0.34%.

So the banks' bigger move came three weeks before the rise, on the day the expectation formed,
not on the day the rise was announced.

### The two days that were different

**7 June 2022: a bigger rise than expected.** Reuters reported that markets had been split between
a rise of 0.25 and 0.40 percentage points. The RBA raised by 0.50. The ASX 200 fell 1.53% and the
four banks fell 2.37% on average, their worst decision day in the run.

**4 October 2022: a smaller rise than expected.** After four rises of 0.50 points in a row, Market
Index reported that the market widely expected a fifth. The RBA raised by 0.25. The ASX 200 rose
3.75% and the four banks rose 4.39% on average, their best decision day in the run. Asia
Financial (Reuters) reported it was the market's best session since June 2020.

Both days had a rate rise. What made them different was how the rise compared with what investors
had expected.

Four rate rises, and four different results. Each bank had the same rate rises; what differed was
what each told investors about its own business this year:

- **ANZ** said it had cut costs sharply: operating expenses fell 9% in the half to March, as part
  of a plan that removes 3,500 roles. Its cash profit rose 14% on the previous half,
  excluding one-off items.
- **CBA** said its cash profit rose 7% to $11.0 billion in the year to June, and that it grew at
  or above the market in all five of its main lending and deposit businesses for the first time.
- **NAB** said its cash profit, excluding a one-off accounting charge for software, rose 2.3% in
  the half to March, and it set aside an extra $300 million for the Middle East conflict.
- **Westpac** said its margin fell 0.06 percentage points to 1.89% in the half to March, naming
  competition as a cause, and its provisions above its central forecast reached $2.0 billion by
  June.

These are the banks' own statements. We report them beside the share prices; we do not claim any
one of them caused a particular move.

### What comes next

**Inflation, Wednesday 28 October, 11:30am.** Inflation is how fast prices are rising. The RBA
raises interest rates to slow it down, and it aims to keep inflation within its target range of 2–3%
a year. On 28 October the ABS (the Australian Bureau of Statistics) reported that inflation is now
**4.3%**, meaning prices in September were 4.3% higher than a year earlier. A month ago, inflation
was **4.0%**. So inflation is above the RBA's target, and it went up rather than down.

**The RBA, Tuesday 3 November, 2:30pm.** Melbourne Cup day. On the evidence above, what matters
for bank shares on the day is less whether rates rise than whether the decision matches what
investors expect by then.

**The banks' own results, the same fortnight.** Westpac reports its full-year results on Monday
2 November, NAB on Thursday 5 November and ANZ on Monday 9 November, according to their financial
calendars. This year, results days moved the banks more than decision days did.

### What this does not tell you

- **Seventeen days is a small sample**, and 13 of them came in one run of rises in 2022–23, which
  started from a cash rate of 0.10%.
- **A share price moves for many reasons at once.** A decision day can also carry company news or
  news from overseas, as 5 May 2026 did.
- **The past is a record, not a forecast.** None of this tells you what bank shares will do on
  3 November.
