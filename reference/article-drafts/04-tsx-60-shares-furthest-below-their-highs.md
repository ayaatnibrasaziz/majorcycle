## The answer, shown under the heading

Ten of the 60 companies in the S&P/TSX 60 sit more than 20% below their own
one-year high, and not one is down more than 40%. Canada is by a wide margin the
calmest of the three markets we cover, and almost all of its damage is in a
single sector.

## Body

### The calmest of the three markets

As at 27 August 2026, the middle company in the S&P/TSX 60 sat **10.2% below its
own highest price of the past year**.

| | ASX 200 | S&P 500 | S&P/TSX 60 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Middle company is below its high by | 17.5% | 11.7% | 10.2% |
| More than 20% below | 44% | 29% | 17% |
| More than 50% below | 16 companies | 12 companies | **none** |
| Deepest single fall | −74.0% | −76.2% | **−39.1%** |

The last row is the one that matters. Australia has sixteen companies more than
half below their own high and the United States has twelve. Canada's worst
performer, Thomson Reuters, is down 39.1%, and nothing else in the index is
below 36%.

Part of that is simply that sixty of a country's largest companies are a
narrower and steadier group than two hundred of them. But it is not only size:
comparing the sixty largest companies in each market, Canada still comes out the
steadiest.

### The ten that are down more than 20%

| # | Company | Below its 1-year high | Below its best since 2000 | Sector label |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Thomson Reuters | −39.1% | −49.3% | Industrials |
| 2 | Open Text | −35.8% | −41.9% | Technology |
| 3 | TELUS | −35.6% | −48.0% | Communication Services |
| 4 | WSP Global | −34.4% | −34.4% | Industrials |
| 5 | Celestica | −32.9% | −32.9% | Technology |
| 6 | Constellation Software | −32.4% | −40.9% | Technology |
| 7 | FirstService | −32.2% | −32.2% | Real Estate |
| 8 | CAE | −27.6% | −27.6% | Industrials |
| 9 | Gildan Activewear | −25.5% | −25.5% | Consumer Cyclical |
| 10 | CGI | −22.9% | −40.6% | Technology |

Four of the ten are technology companies, and Canada only has five technology
companies in this index. The fifth, Shopify, is 15.6% below its high. **Every
single technology company in the S&P/TSX 60 is meaningfully below its own peak,
and four of the five are down more than 20%.**

The two sector labels that would mislead you here are the first and the fifth.
Thomson Reuters is filed under Industrials but sells legal and financial
information, and Celestica is filed under Technology but manufactures
electronics to order. If you read the sector column as a description of the
business, you would draw the wrong conclusion about both.

### Everything Canada is famous for is near its high

The mirror image of that concentration is how completely untouched the rest of
the index is.

- **Five of the six banks** are within 8% of their own highs — Royal Bank 6.9%
  below, Toronto-Dominion 4.3%, Bank of Nova Scotia 2.2%, Bank of Montreal and
  CIBC 7.9% each — and the sixth, National Bank, is 10.2% below.
- **All five gold companies** are near their peaks: Franco-Nevada 2.7% below,
  Wheaton 3.4%, Barrick 10.2%, Agnico Eagle 14.5%, Kinross 15.5%.
- **Every oil and gas producer** is within 11%: Canadian Natural 2.6% below,
  Suncor 4.0%, Imperial Oil 4.2%, Cenovus 4.4%, Tourmaline 10.6%.
- The two copper miners, Teck and First Quantum, are 2.0% and 1.8% below —
  First Quantum is the only company in the whole index within 2% of its high.

Not one company in financial services, energy, basic materials, consumer
staples or utilities is more than 20% below its high. Between them those five
groups are 38 of the 60.

This is a very unusual shape for a market to be in. The parts of the Canadian
index that people think of as cyclical and risky — banks, oil, mining — are the
parts holding up, and the damage sits almost entirely in software and services.

### What the history says about falls this size

Because nothing in this index has fallen more than 40%, the relevant history is
the shallow end of the range, and it is comparatively reassuring. Measuring
every 20% fall in the TSX 60 since 2000:

| The size of the fall | Falls | Back within 5 years | Still down 5 years on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20% to 30% | 195 | 100% | 0% |
| 30% to 40% | 74 | 100% | 0% |

Every Canadian fall of between 20% and 40% that has had five years to recover,
has recovered. Not one exception in 206 of them. That is a completely different
picture from a fall past 70%, where only about a third were back inside five
years.

It is not a promise. Those are 269 past falls in a specific set of large
companies, not a rule about the future, and eleven Canadian falls are below their
old price as we write — all of them recent. But the size of a fall is the
strongest single predictor we have found of how it ends, and on that measure
Canada's ten are in the mildest part of the range.

The same measurement across all three markets is in
[How long does an ASX share take to recover?](/articles/how-long-does-an-asx-share-take-to-recover),
and the two companion rankings are
[the ASX 200](/articles/asx-200-shares-furthest-below-their-highs) and
[the S&P 500](/articles/sp-500-shares-furthest-below-their-highs).

### How to read the numbers

The one-year high is the highest price actually traded in the past 252 trading
days. Prices include dividends, which matters in Canada, where the banks and
pipelines pay a large part of their return that way. Figures are as at 27 August
2026. Nothing here is advice about any company.

## Fact-check sheet

### Method

Identical to the other two ranking pieces: the 60 current S&P/TSX 60 members,
prices from 1 January 2000 to 27 August 2026, the one-year drawdown calculated
exactly as the site's own stock pages calculate it, prices adjusted for
dividends and splits. Inputs frozen in
`reference/drawdown-recovery-2026-08-27-DATA.json.gz`.

### Checks I ran

| What I checked | What I found |
|---|---|
| Is the whole index really shown? | Yes — all 60 lines, no duplicate share classes, no member missing price history. This is the only one of the three articles where nothing is selected out. |
| Is any price history corrupted? | No. Every one-day fall of 22% or more across all 761 companies and 26 years — 1,122 of them — was checked against the provider's split calendar. The one artifact found was Monster Beverage, a US company. |
| Are the "nobody is down" claims right? | Checked company by company rather than from the sector totals. Financial services 12, energy 9, basic materials 8, consumer defensive 5, utilities 4 — 38 companies, none more than 20% down. |
| Does any TSX 60 company have no sector? | One: **National Bank of Canada**, which carries no sector label in the provider's data. I counted it as a bank by hand, which is why the article says six banks and the sector table would say five. Article 1 hit the same gap with the same company. |
| **Do our prices lag the market?** | **They did, and it is FIXED — found in this audit and corrected before publishing (2026-08-30).** Our stored price history was never re-adjusted for a company's most recent dividend, so the older bars — the peak included — sat 1.7% to 3.5% high and every fall read up to two points deeper than the truth. The cause is now fixed in the nightly pipeline (a dividend triggers a full re-pull, exactly as a split already did) and the whole history of all 871 companies was re-pulled once to clear the accumulated drift. Verified afterwards on 24 companies sampled at random across the three markets — 253,000 prices, **zero** still drifting. Every figure in this article is computed from the corrected data, so the article, the stock pages and any outside source a reader checks now agree. **81 of the 464 checked figures moved**, which is why you are reviewing these again. |
| Are the sector labels safe to describe? | No, and I say so in the body. Thomson Reuters is filed under Industrials and Celestica under Technology, and neither label describes the business. Naming them is the fix. |
| Is Canada's steadiness just because it is 60 companies? | Partly, and I say so. Against the sixty largest in each of the other markets Canada is still the steadiest, so it is not only a sample-size effect. |
| Did I check the small numbers or estimate them? | One was estimated and wrong. I wrote that twelve Canadian falls of 20–40% are under water today, by taking 2.6% of 195 and 8.1% of 74. Counting them gives **eleven**. Corrected, and every other figure in all four articles is now asserted against the frozen data file by a script rather than worked out by hand. |
| Is the 5-year recovery figure of 100% real? | Yes, on 153 and 53 qualifying falls. It is a small sample by the standards of the S&P 500 and the article says the number of falls in the table so a reader can judge that. |
