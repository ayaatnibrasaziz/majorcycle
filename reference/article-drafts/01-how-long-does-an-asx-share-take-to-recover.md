## The answer, shown under the heading

About half of all 20% falls in the ASX 200 were back to the old price within a
year, and three quarters within three years. One in eight is still below its old
price today — and almost all of the falls that stay down for years are the very
deepest ones.

## Body

### Half get back inside a year

We took the 201 companies in the ASX 200 today and read every daily price back to
January 2000. Whenever a company's share price dropped 20% or more below its own
previous high, we started a clock and stopped it on the day the price got back to
that high.

That happened **1,260 times** across **198** of the 201 companies. Only three
have never once been 20% below their own high — Coles, Dalrymple Bay
Infrastructure and The Lottery Corporation, all of which listed recently enough
that they have not yet met a bad year.

Of those 1,260 falls:

| Back to the old price within | Share of falls |
|---|---|
| 1 year | 51.7% |
| 2 years | 68.5% |
| 3 years | 75.9% |
| 5 years | 84.4% |
| 10 years | 91.2% |

Each row only counts falls that have had that much time to run. A fall that
started in 2024 cannot tell you anything about ten-year recoveries, so it is left
out of the ten-year row rather than counted as a failure. The rows are built from
1,170 falls, then 1,107, 1,072, 946 and 650.

**And 156 falls, 12.4% of them, are still below their old price today.** That
number needs care, because most of those are simply recent. Sixty-three of the 156
began less than a year ago and have not had time to do anything yet. The ones
worth worrying about are the old ones: **45 falls, 3.6% of the total, have been
under water for more than five years.**

### The depth of the fall decides almost everything

The single most useful thing in this study is not the average. It is how fast
the odds change with the size of the fall.

| How far it fell | Falls | Typical wait | Back within 5 years | Still down 5 years on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20% to 30% | 489 | 4 months | 100% | 0% |
| 30% to 40% | 254 | 9 months | 99% | 1% |
| 40% to 50% | 157 | 1.5 years | 94% | 6% |
| 50% to 70% | 190 | 2.9 years | 73% | 27% |
| More than 70% | 170 | 8.3 years | 33% | 67% |

The last two columns are the same falls counted two ways, so they add to 100%.
They cover only the falls old enough to be judged — those with a full five years
of history behind them, which is 376 of the 489 shallow ones down to 153 of the
deepest.

A 25% fall is close to routine: every single one that had five years to recover,
did. Not one exception in 376. A fall past 70% is a different animal — two thirds
of those were still below the old price five years later.

This is also where the "still under water" falls are concentrated. Of the 45
Australian falls that have been under water for more than five years, **38 of
them are falls of more than 70%**, and not a single one is a fall of less than
40%.

The useful way to read this is that the first half of a big fall and the second
half are not the same event. Going from down 20% to down 50% roughly doubles the
wait. Going from down 50% to down 70% quadruples it.

### Australian shares take longer than American ones

Australia is the slowest of the three markets we cover, on both measures.

| | Typical wait | Back within 1 year | Still down today | Down over 5 years |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ASX 200 | about 11 months | 51.7% | 12.4% | 3.6% |
| S&P 500 | about 10 months | 54.1% | 8.5% | 0.8% |
| TSX 60 | about 10 months | 55.9% | 6.2% | 0.3% |

The last column is the one that separates the three markets. Australia has 45
falls that have stayed under water for more than five years; the United States,
with three times as many falls in the sample, has 32.

The obvious suspicion is that this is really about company size, and in
[How far do ASX shares actually fall?](/articles/how-far-do-asx-shares-fall) that
turned out to be the whole story: Australian shares look like they fall harder
than American ones, and once you compare the sixty biggest in each, the gap
reverses.

**Here it does not reverse.** Comparing the sixty largest companies in each
index, the Australian ones still take longer and still leave more falls
unrecovered — a typical wait of 0.82 years against 0.71 in the United States,
and 10.1% still down against 7.1%. Matching for size narrows the gap. It does
not close it.

### Property is where falls go to die

Inside the Australian market, the differences between industries are far larger
than the difference between countries.

| Group | Companies | Typical wait | Back within 5 years | Still down today |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Miners | 50 | 6 months | 84% | 11.8% |
| Banks | 7 | 1.4 years | 84% | 14.0% |
| Listed property | 19 | 2.9 years | 61% | 19.6% |

Miners fall the furthest of any Australian group, which is what
[How far do ASX shares actually fall?](/articles/how-far-do-asx-shares-fall)
measured, and they also climb back the fastest. Commodity prices turn, and
the share price turns with them.

Property does the opposite. Eleven property falls of 20% or more are still below
their old price, and four of those have been that way for more than five years —
the highest rate of any Australian group. The GPT Group peaked in February 2007, fell 93%, and is still about
18% below that price more than nineteen years later. Lendlease peaked in August
2018 and now trades 83% below it. The reason is that property trusts answer a
crash by issuing new shares, often at a heavy discount, and an investor who owned
the shares before that cannot get back to where they started no matter what the
buildings are worth afterwards.

### What "recovered" means here, exactly

Two things are worth being precise about, because they change the answer.

**Dividends count.** Our prices are adjusted for dividends, so "back to the old
price" means back to where you started including the income along the way. That
matters most for the highest-yielding shares. Across the largest ASX companies,
86.7% of falls recovered on that basis against 81.8% on the share price alone.
Westpac's typical fall took 2.1 years to recover with dividends counted and 5.1
years without. If you check one of these against a price chart,
the chart will show a later date, because the chart does not show the income.

**We measure from the day it was 20% down**, not from the peak — that is the day
a holder actually notices, and the more useful clock.

### What this cannot tell you

These are the 201 companies in the ASX 200 **today**. Companies that fell and
were dropped from the index are not here, and they did worse than the ones that
stayed. So read this as "what the history of today's large Australian companies
looks like", not as the odds facing any share you might buy. The true
never-recovered figure across all Australian shares is certainly worse than
12.8%.

It is also a description of the past, and the past has no obligation to repeat.
Nothing here is advice about any particular company.

## Fact-check sheet

### Method

- **Universe:** the 201 current members of the S&P/ASX 200, the 500 of the S&P
  500 and the 60 of the S&P/TSX 60 — **761 companies**. Membership and company
  details are frozen in `reference/drawdown-recovery-2026-08-27-DATA.json.gz`.
- **Window:** 1 January 2000 to **27 August 2026**, one date for every company.
- **A "fall":** the share price leaves its own running high, drops at least 20%
  below it on a closing basis, and (maybe) returns to that high. Falls do not
  overlap — one fall runs until the old high is regained.
- **The clock:** starts the first day the close is 20% or more below the peak;
  stops the first day the close is back at or above it.
- **Unfinished falls are kept**, not dropped. The typical wait uses a
  Kaplan-Meier estimate, which is the standard way to take an average when some
  cases have not finished. Dropping them would have made the ASX figure look
  like 8 months instead of 11.
- **Prices are adjusted for dividends and share splits.**
- **The dividend comparison is a separate measurement** and is the only figure in
  this article not derived from the frozen file. For the 40 largest ASX companies
  I pulled both the dividend-adjusted and the price-only series from the provider
  and ran the identical calculation on each: 86.7% of falls recovered against
  81.8%, and the per-company median wait was 0.81 years against 0.97. The control
  is that a company which has never paid a dividend must return identical results
  on both series — four did (Block, Lynas Rare Earths, Xero and Capstone Copper),
  and all four matched exactly.

### Checks I ran on myself before writing

| What I checked | What I found |
|---|---|
| Do all 761 companies share one date? | Friday 28 August's refresh **stopped halfway** — 260 of 761 had no bar for it. Everything is pinned to 27 August, the last date all 761 share. Reported separately. |
| Is any price history corrupted? | **Yes — one company.** Monster Beverage's history interleaves pre- and post-split prices around its 2:1 split of 11 August 2026, making it look 53% down when it is about 7% down. It is excluded from all four articles and the S&P 500 counts read 499. It is the only such case in 26 years and 761 companies. |
| Are the big one-day moves real? | Checked every one-day fall of 22% or more — 1,122 of them across 344 companies — against the provider's split calendar. Only Monster's was an artifact. Cochlear (−41% in a day), Tuas (−63%) and Moderna (+177%) are real: each traded 20 to 50 times its normal volume that day, which a corporate action does not cause. |
| Does the result depend on single bad price points? | Re-ran everything on a 3-day smoothed price. The typical ASX wait moves from 0.94 to 1.13 years — which is why the article says "about a year" and not a precise figure. The rankings, the depth ladder and the never-recovered shares barely move. |
| **Do our prices lag the market?** | **They did, and it is FIXED — found in this audit and corrected before publishing (2026-08-30).** Our stored price history was never re-adjusted for a company's most recent dividend, so the older bars — the peak included — sat 1.7% to 3.5% high and every fall read up to two points deeper than the truth. The cause is now fixed in the nightly pipeline (a dividend triggers a full re-pull, exactly as a split already did) and the whole history of all 871 companies was re-pulled once to clear the accumulated drift. Verified afterwards on 24 companies sampled at random across the three markets — 253,000 prices, **zero** still drifting. Every figure in this article is computed from the corrected data, so the article, the stock pages and any outside source a reader checks now agree. **81 of the 464 checked figures moved**, which is why you are reviewing these again. |
| Is the Australia-vs-US gap really about size? | This is the check article 1 taught us to run. It is not: the gap survives matching the sixty largest in each market. |
| Is "Basic Materials" the same as "miners"? | No, and article 1 established the exact list. Six of the fifty are not miners (BlueScope, Sims, James Hardie, Fletcher Building, Orica, Dyno Nobel) and six real miners sit under Energy. The corrected group of 50 is used here. |
