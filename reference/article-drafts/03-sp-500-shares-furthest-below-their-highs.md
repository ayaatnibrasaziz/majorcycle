## Body

### An index near its high, holding a lot of companies that are not

As at 27 August 2026, the middle company in the S&P 500 sat **11.7% below its own
highest price of the past year**. Twenty-five companies were within 2% of their
high.

| How far below its own one-year high | Companies |
|---|---|
| More than 10% | 283 of 499 |
| More than 20% | 143 |
| More than 30% | 63 |
| More than 50% | 12 |
| Within 2% of the high | 25 |

That is a much healthier picture than
[the ASX 200](/articles/asx-200-shares-furthest-below-their-highs), where 44% of
the index is more than 20% down against 29% here. But the American average conceals a bigger
split than the Australian one.

Two things stand out. The first is how many are household names rather than
speculative small companies: Oracle, Nike, Intuit, Boston Scientific and Charter
are among the largest businesses in the United States.

The second is the gap between the columns. Nike is 50% below its one-year high
and 77% below its 2021 peak, so this year's fall is the latest instalment of a
four-year decline rather than a new event. Oracle is the opposite: the two
figures are identical because Oracle's highest price ever was September 2025.
It went up a very long way and gave all of it back inside twelve months.

The obvious reading is that this is an artificial intelligence unwind, and the
obvious reading is wrong. The companies most associated with that trade are
holding up: Nvidia is 3.5% below its high, Microsoft 8.0%, Apple 8.6%, Amazon
10.8%. The damage is in the tier below them. Oracle is down 56%, Intuit 50%,
Intel 35%.

Of the fifty largest listings in the index, fourteen are more than 20% below
their own high — so this is not only a small-company story either. Meta is 27.5% below,
Tesla 28.9%, Broadcom 24.8%.

Meanwhile the oldest parts of the market are at their peaks. Visa is 1.5% below
its high, Mastercard 1.6%, Marathon Petroleum 1.1%. Only two of twenty-one
energy companies and three of thirty-one utilities are down more than 20%.

Those three utilities are worth a sentence, because they are the exception that
explains the rule. NRG, Vistra and Constellation Energy are independent power
producers, and each had more than doubled in the two years before this fall —
Vistra was up 162% and is now 36% below its high. They fell furthest because
they had risen furthest, which is a different thing from a sector in trouble.

### What the history says about shares in this position

[How long does an ASX share take to recover?](/articles/how-long-does-an-asx-share-take-to-recover)
measured every 20% fall in these indices since 2000 and how long it took to come
back. For the S&P 500, 54% of falls were back within a year and 90% within five,
and **8.5% are still below their old price today**.

American falls have recovered faster and more reliably than Australian ones. The
clearest version of that is the long stragglers: 0.8% of American falls have been
under water for more than five years against 3.6% of Australian ones, and the gap
survives matching the two markets for company size.

But the base rate that matters for the table above is the one for deep falls
rather than average ones. Within the S&P 500 itself:

| The size of the fall | Falls | Back within 5 years | Still down 5 years on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20% to 30% | 1,630 | 100% | 0% |
| 30% to 40% | 877 | 99% | 1% |
| 40% to 50% | 512 | 97% | 3% |
| 50% to 70% | 537 | 79% | 21% |
| More than 70% | 296 | 34% | 66% |

The last two columns are the same falls counted two ways and add to 100%. Both
use only the falls with five full years of history behind them.

America's advantage sits in the shallow and middle falls, not the deep ones. Once
a company has fallen more than 70% the three markets look almost identical: about
a third are back within five years in each of them, and the American edge only
reappears over ten years, at 61% against Australia's 56%.

Every company in the fifteen above has fallen more than 48%. Whatever else is
true of them, they are in the part of the distribution where the outcomes are
widest — both the best recoveries and the permanent losses live there. A list
like this is a place to start reading company filings, not a conclusion.

### How to read the numbers

The one-year high is the highest price actually traded in the past 252 trading
days; for a few companies here it was set on a single day. Prices include
dividends. Figures are as at 27 August 2026 and will be out of date the moment
the market opens again. Nothing here is advice about any company.

## Fact-check sheet

### Method

Identical to the ASX 200 piece: the 500 current S&P 500 members, prices from
1 January 2000 to 27 August 2026, the one-year drawdown calculated exactly as
the site's own stock pages calculate it, prices adjusted for dividends and
splits. Inputs frozen in `reference/drawdown-recovery-2026-08-27-DATA.json.gz`.

### Checks I ran, and the one that changed the article

| What I checked | What I found |
|---|---|
| **Is any price history corrupted?** | **Yes, and it was in this index.** Monster Beverage appeared in my first draft at ninth place, 53.5% below its high. It is not. The provider's price history interleaves pre- and post-split prices around Monster's 2-for-1 split of 11 August 2026 — 17 bars between 13 July and 7 August sit at the unsplit price. Monster is about 7% below its high. It is **excluded**, and every S&P count in this article reads 499 rather than 500. |
| Why did my first check miss it? | Because I compared our database against the data provider, and both carry the same defect. A check that cannot see the fault reports exactly what a clean system reports. I found it instead by looking for a signature no correct price series has: a 50% fall that reverses within days. |
| Are there others? | No. Every one-day fall of 22% or more across all 761 companies and 26 years — 1,122 of them — was tested against the provider's split calendar. Monster is the only one. |
| Are the other big one-day moves real? | Yes. Fiserv fell 44% on 29 October 2025 and Centene 40% on 2 July 2025; each traded many times its normal volume that day, which a split does not cause. |
| **Do our prices lag the market?** | **They did, and it is FIXED — found in this audit and corrected before publishing (2026-08-30).** Our stored price history was never re-adjusted for a company's most recent dividend, so the older bars — the peak included — sat 1.7% to 3.5% high and every fall read up to two points deeper than the truth. The cause is now fixed in the nightly pipeline (a dividend triggers a full re-pull, exactly as a split already did) and the whole history of all 871 companies was re-pulled once to clear the accumulated drift. Verified afterwards on 24 companies sampled at random across the three markets — 253,000 prices, **zero** still drifting. Every figure in this article is computed from the corrected data, so the article, the stock pages and any outside source a reader checks now agree. **81 of the 464 checked figures moved**, which is why you are reviewing these again. |
| Is the sector table safe to describe? | The labels are the provider's classification, not a description. Where I make a claim about what a company does — the three power producers — I name the companies so you can check them. |
| Does any S&P 500 company have no sector? | One: Fiserv. It appears in the ranking and is left out of the sector table only. |
| Is the index 500 companies or 503? | Our membership list holds **503 lines**. Three of them — the tickers FDXF, HONA and Q — are recent additions with no row in our stocks table and no price history at all, leaving **500 lines** in the study and **499** after excluding Monster. Those 500 lines include two share classes each for Alphabet, Fox and News Corporation, so they represent **497 distinct companies**. Alphabet's two lines both appear in the ranking, 16.6% and 16.5% below their highs. |
